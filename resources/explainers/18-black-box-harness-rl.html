Training Through a Harness You Can't See Inside

ClawGym II: Exploring Black-Box RL on Agent Harness · Renmin University of China + IQuest · 17 Aug 2026 · read the source
How to improve a model's use of Claude Code without ever opening Claude Code

A production harness can be treated as a sealed box and trained through anyway — watch the model calls crossing the wire, and rebuild the session from them. What you rebuild is not a transcript. It's a tree — and the branch points are the harness's own compaction and subagent features.

01The problem it exists to solve

Suppose you want a model to get better at being driven by Claude Code. The standard way to improve a model at a task is to let it attempt the task many times, score the results, and shift its weights toward whatever produced good scores. That requires two things: the exact sequence of tokens the model produced, and a score.

A real harness gives you neither cleanly. It's a large program you didn't write, it rewrites the conversation as it goes, and what leaks out of it is — in the paper's words — "fragmented, forked, and often redundant model calls" rather than a tidy start-to-finish transcript.

What researchers did instead

  • Wrote their own simple agent loop with a handful of tools
  • Trained against that, because every step was visible
  • Shipped the model into a real harness and hoped it transferred

What this paper does

  • Runs the real, unmodified harness in a sandbox
  • Watches only the model endpoint — the one boundary everything crosses
  • Reconstructs the branched session from those calls alone
Why this repo cares, given it trains nothing. The reconstruction is the most precise published account of what a harness does to the shape of a session — written by people who had to infer it from outside, with no source access. That's a description you can't get from the harness's own docs.

02How it works

Two moves. Capture at the boundary — however opaque the harness is, every model action must cross the wire to the model, so a proxy sitting there sees all of the model's behaviour (and none of the harness's reasoning about it). Then rebuild by longest prefix — attach each captured call to whichever existing node's accumulated history is the longest prefix of that call's input.

The subtlety that generalises furthest. Harnesses normalise tool calls and re-serialise assistant messages. Rebuild the trajectory from what the transcript says and you encode the harness's cleaned-up version, not what the model emitted. So the paper keeps two views: generated tokens go to training verbatim, decoded text goes to the harness, and the harness's edits "stay on its own view." Restated for everyone else: the transcript is a rendering, not the record.

03How much to believe it

This is a measured paper — real runs, reported conditions, ablations across two harnesses and two algorithms. That puts it above most of this pool. But the tables have a specific weakness, and it is worth knowing before you look at them.

The word doing the work is "slightly." With no variance reported, a sub-2-point gap is not distinguishable from zero. Read this as "no evident degradation" — which is what the authors mostly say — not as a gain.
Seeds, error bars, variance
Not disclosed
Every cell in every table is a single run. There is no repeated evaluation anywhere in the paper, so the noise floor is unknown. This is the load-bearing caveat for everything below.
The rule this gives you. Cite the headline gains freely; they are big. Treat any difference under roughly two points as absent. And remember n=30 for PinchBench — thirty tasks, after discarding the multimodal ones.

Those last two tiles are the white-box comparison, and they invert cleanly: each model wins in the harness it was trained in, and loses in the other one. Training in a transparent hand-built loop does transfer — it beats the untrained baseline by 5.22 in OpenClaw — but stays 12.29 points behind the model fitted to OpenClaw directly.

05The one thing to remember

Context compaction or subagent execution may start a new segment from a shortened or alternative context, after which subsequent calls continue to extend the newly established history.

A session is a tree, not a transcript — and its branch points are exactly the context-management features you chose to turn on. Compaction isn't a lossy edit to one long conversation; it ends a segment and starts a new root. A subagent isn't a helper inside your session; it's a separate branch.

The corollary is uncomfortable and the paper states it plainly: those auxiliary branches are excluded from training entirely, because the task's final reward can't be fairly assigned to them — it "would introduce ambiguous credit assignment." So the harness's most sophisticated moves are precisely the ones nothing can score.

06What we take from it

Take

  • The tree model of a session — the vocabulary change is free and permanent
  • "The transcript is a rendering, not the record" — true well beyond RL
  • Over-branching as a stuck-agent detector, computed from call structure alone
  • Settling: request completion is not record completion
  • Numbers for how much harness choice costs, when nothing else in the pool has any

Leave

  • All of the RL machinery — PPO, GRPO, advantage estimation. Nothing here trains models
  • The mix-harness result — inside the noise band
  • Any harness-vs-harness ranking from the trained rows
  • The infrastructure specifics — sandbox provisioning, importance-sampling correction
Where it disagrees with us. Resource 02 says "a decent model with a great harness beats a great model with a bad harness," treating the model as fixed and the harness as the thing you tune. This paper measures that and it survives — but adds a condition 02 doesn't carry: the fit is itself trainable, and it doesn't transfer. The unit of engineering is the (model, harness) pair, not the harness alone. Against resource 01, compaction gets a cost 01 never prices: it's the thing that severs the run.
The gap it touches — G7, "never written the agent loop yourself." It doesn't close G7 and won't help you build a loop. What it adds is the trade-off G7 was missing: owning the loop buys you a better training environment and a worse deployment fit. Read §4.6 before deciding how much to invest there.
